3
Three equations everyone has seen: heat, Maxwell, incompressible flow. They are written with grad, curl and div. The right-hand card is what the operators satisfy, and it is where discretizations go wrong: a scheme that does not keep curl grad = 0 has spurious modes in electromagnetics; one that does not keep the divergence theorem leaks mass. Order of accuracy does not fix that. The rest of this part is a calculus that keeps these identities by construction.

The Hodge Star Carries the Metric

\(\star\colon \Omega^k \to \Omega^{n-k}\) pairs each quantity with its dual: a gradient along a curve with a flux across it.
\(d\): topology
Needs only what is the boundary of what. No lengths, no angles, no material constants.
Conservation and balance laws live here.
\(\star\): geometry
Carries all of the metric, and with a coefficient, the material.
Constitutive laws live here:
\(\phi = -k\star dC,\quad D = \varepsilon\star E\)
\[ \delta = (-1)^{k}\,\star^{-1} d\,\star \]
\[ \nabla^2 C = {\star}d{\star}d\,C = -\delta d\,C \]
\[ \partial_t C = k\,{\star}d{\star}d\,C \]
Physics splits into a metric-free part, \(d\), and a metric part, \(\star\).
6
d needs no lengths or angles: it only knows what is the boundary of what. The Hodge star is where the metric enters. It turns a k-form into the (n-k)-form for the same quantity, for instance a gradient along a curve into a flux across it. Constitutive laws are Hodge stars weighted by a material constant: Fick, Fourier, Ohm, D = epsilon E. The Laplacian is star d star d on functions, so diffusion is written with d and star alone. The split between the two columns is what DEC keeps when it discretizes.

Discrete Forms Are Cochains

A discrete \(k\)-form is one number per oriented \(k\)-simplex: the integral of the smooth form over it, \(\langle R\,\omega, \sigma\rangle = \int_\sigma \omega\).
0-form: values at vertices
1-form: integrals along edges
2-form: integrals over faces
The edge numbers are differences of the vertex numbers: this 1-form is \(d\) of the 0-form.
Store integrals, not point samples, and the theorems of calculus hold exactly.
[1] A. N. Hirani. Discrete exterior calculus, PhD thesis, California Institute of Technology, 2003 · [2]
7
Replace space by a simplicial mesh. A discrete k-form stores one number on each oriented k-simplex, and that number is the integral of the smooth form over the simplex: values at vertices, line integrals along edges, fluxes through triangles. Flipping an edge's orientation flips the sign of its number. The edge numbers here are the differences of the vertex numbers: that 1-form is d of the 0-form. Because the unknowns are integrals, not samples at points, the theorems of the calculus hold exactly; that is the next slide.

The Discrete Derivative Is the Coboundary

  • ●\(d\,R = R\,d\): discretizing commutes with \(d\)
  • ●Fluxes telescope: conservation to round-off
Definition. Stokes' theorem, taken as the definition:
\[ \langle d\omega, \sigma\rangle \triangleq \langle \omega, \partial\sigma\rangle, \quad\text{so}\quad d_k = \partial_{k+1}^{\top} \]
\[ d_0 = \begin{array}{c|ccc} & 1 & 2 & 3 \\ \hline [12] & -1 & 1 & 0 \\ [23] & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ [13] & -1 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \]
\[ d_1 = \begin{array}{c|ccc} & [12] & [23] & [13] \\ \hline [123] & 1 & 1 & -1 \end{array} \]

[1] A. N. Hirani. Discrete exterior calculus, PhD thesis, California Institute of Technology, 2003 · [2]
8
Define d by Stokes' theorem: the integral of d omega over a simplex is the integral of omega over its boundary. Then d is the transpose of the boundary matrix, a signed incidence matrix. For one triangle, d0 takes vertex values to edge differences and d1 sums the edges around the face with their signs. The product is zero, exactly, in integers, on every mesh, because the boundary of a boundary is empty. This is the same incidence matrix as in the graph and sheaf talks; d0 is the graph's.

\(\star_0\)

9
The circumcentric dual: each triangle's circumcenter is a dual vertex, and each primal edge has a dual edge through it at right angles, joining the circumcenters on either side. A k-simplex gets an (n-k)-dimensional dual cell. The discrete Hodge star is diagonal: the ratio of the dual cell's measure to the primal cell's. On an edge that ratio is the cotangent weight. All the metric information in DEC is in these diagonals. Once a triangle is obtuse its circumcenter leaves it, and the dual lengths have to be signed for the weights to stay right. Our diagonal star uses unsigned lengths, so it equals the cotangent weight only when no triangle is obtuse; the next slide is what the solvers use instead.

a third of each adjacent triangle's area
\(\star_1\)

10
What our solvers use. The barycentric dual puts each dual vertex at a triangle's centroid, so every dual cell stays inside the mesh whatever the triangles look like; the dual edge of a primal edge bends at the edge's midpoint. On this dual the diagonal star is no longer right, so CombinatorialSpaces and Decapodes default to the Hodge star of Ayoub, Hamdouni and Razafindralandy. In each triangle, the dual piece of an edge makes an angle theta with the edge. The sine term is the diagonal part; the cosine term brings in the triangle's other two edges, through the coefficients that write the rotated edge in terms of them. On the circumcentric dual theta is ninety degrees and this is the diagonal star of the last slide. It is exact on piecewise-constant forms for any choice of centers, which is why d0 transpose star1 d0 is the cotangent stiffness matrix exactly, obtuse triangles included; we checked that against the library to round-off. The 0- and 2-form stars stay diagonal; the dual cell of a vertex has a third of the area of each triangle around it.

The Discrete de Rham Complex

primal: vertices, edges, triangles
\[ \begin{CD} \Omega^0 @>{d_0}>> \Omega^1 @>{d_1}>> \Omega^2 \\ @V{\star_0}VV @V{\star_1}VV @V{\star_2}VV \\ \tilde\Omega^2 @<{\tilde d_1}<< \tilde\Omega^1 @<{\tilde d_0}<< \tilde\Omega^0 \end{CD} \]
dual: cells, edges, vertices
Rows: topology, exact
\[ \tilde d_0 = d_1^{\top}, \qquad \tilde d_1 = -d_0^{\top} \]
\(d\circ d = 0\) along both rows, in integers
Columns: geometry, approximate
The Hodge stars are the only arrows between the rows, and the only place the metric enters.
Around the first square:
\[ \nabla^2 = \star_0^{-1}\, \tilde d_1\, \star_1\, d_0 \]
Every operator is a path through this diagram.
11
Put the two meshes together. The top row is the primal complex: values on vertices, edges and triangles, joined by d0 and d1. The bottom row is the dual complex, running the other way: dual vertices, dual edges and dual cells. Its derivatives are the primal ones transposed, d-tilde-0 = d1 transpose and d-tilde-1 = minus d0 transpose, so the bottom row is exact for the same reason the top row is. The minus sign is CombinatorialSpaces following the continuum exterior calculus sign convention. The Hodge stars are the only vertical arrows, and the only place the metric enters. Every operator in the model is a path through this diagram: the Laplacian on functions goes down the first square, d0 across, star1 down, d-tilde-1 back, and star0 inverse up. With d-tilde-1 = minus d0 transpose and positive stars, that product is minus star0 inverse d0 transpose star1 d0: the Laplace-Beltrami operator, div grad, the L of the next slide. It matches the library to round-off, although the library calls the negative of it the Laplace-Beltrami.

Compose the pieces. The Laplacian on functions is star d star d; with the discrete matrices it is the product on the slide. Written out at a vertex it is the cotangent Laplacian, whose stiffness part is exactly the stiffness matrix of linear finite elements, with a lumped mass. Set every Hodge star to the identity and what is left is d0 transpose d0, the graph Laplacian: the operator in the consensus and sheaf work is this one with the geometry taken out.

What Is Exact and What Is Approximate

Exact, from topology
  • ●\(d \circ d = 0\)
  • ●Stokes' theorem on every chain of cells
  • ●Conservation: what leaves one cell enters its neighbor
  • ●Cohomology: harmonic forms count the holes of the mesh
Approximate, from geometry
  • ●The Hodge star \(\star\); positive weights need a Delaunay mesh (in 2D)
  • ●The wedge product \(\wedge\), associative only on closed forms
  • ●Interior product \(\iota_X\) and Lie derivative \(\mathcal{L}_X\)
  • ●Sharp \(\sharp\) and flat \(\flat\), between forms and vector fields
Topology is exact; only the metric is approximated.
[1] A. N. Hirani. Discrete exterior calculus, PhD thesis, California Institute of Technology, 2003 · [2, 4]
13
The ledger. Everything built from d alone is exact on any mesh: d d = 0, Stokes, conservation, and the topology, since the kernel of the discrete Hodge Laplacian counts the holes in the mesh just as in the smooth theory. Error enters only through the metric: the Hodge star, and the operators built from it and from interpolation, the wedge, interior and Lie derivatives, and the maps between forms and vector fields. Finite element exterior calculus makes the same split with Whitney forms in place of the diagonal stars.

Each failure on the last slide has a history and a standard engineering fix, and each fix restores one identity by hand. Checkerboards: the MAC method staggered pressure and velocity from 1965, and SIMPLE kept it, but body-fitted and unstructured meshes in the 1980s pushed codes back to collocated storage. Rhie-Chow momentum interpolation, 1983, adds a pressure-diffusion term that damps the odd-even mode; it is in most commercial collocated codes, and its answer can depend on the time step and under-relaxation. In finite elements the same failure is the inf-sup or LBB condition, fixed with stable element pairs or PSPG stabilization. Spurious currents: the continuum surface force model made multiphase codes practical and gave them parasitic currents around a static droplet; shallow water and weather codes meet the same thing as the lake at rest. The fixes, balanced-force algorithms and well-balanced schemes, compute the force and the pressure gradient with the same operator at the same place. Energy growth: Arakawa's 1966 Jacobian conserved energy and enstrophy to stop the nonlinear instability of long weather runs; summation-by-parts operators give the discrete integration by parts that provable stability needs. The everyday fix is artificial dissipation, which hides the growth. Circulation: potential flow cannot fix the circulation, so panel methods add vortex sheets and a Kutta condition per lifting body, and full-potential codes add a wake cut. The last line names the common idea: staggering for Maxwell, support operators, DEC and finite element exterior calculus are one approach, and DEC has all four properties.

Spurious Currents in a Fluid at Rest

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_t u + \operatorname{grad} p &= \nu\,\Delta u - \operatorname{grad}\Phi \\ \operatorname{div} u &= 0 \end{aligned} \]
exact solution: \(u = 0\), \(p = -\Phi\)
Take the curl
\[ \partial_t \omega = \nu\,\Delta\omega - \operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad}\Phi \]
DEC: \(d_1 d_0 \Phi = 0\). Collocated: not zero, so vorticity is forced.
\(d_1 d_0 = 0\): no spurious vorticity, and mass conserved cell by cell.
16
d1 d0 = 0 in a simulation. Incompressible Stokes flow in a closed box, at rest under a conservative body force, minus the gradient of a potential well. The exact answer is that nothing moves: the pressure balances the force. Both runs use the same mesh, the same projection and the same implicit viscous solve, built from the diagonal barycentric Hodge stars. The only difference is how the force reaches the edges. Left: the usual collocated practice, least-squares gradients at the nodes averaged onto each edge. That force is not exactly a gradient, the projection cannot remove the remainder, and the fluid spins up into spurious vortices, to a speed of 0.25. This is the static-droplet parasitic current, or the lake-at-rest test, in its simplest form. Right: DEC, where the force is minus d0 Phi; its curl is d1 d0 Phi = 0, the projection removes all of it, and the fluid stays at rest to 1e-14. Viscosity 0.01, colour is vorticity. The curl of the momentum equation shows why: the only vorticity source is the curl of the discrete gradient of Phi. Read the same identity with a stream function and it says the edge fluxes d0 psi leave no net mass in any triangle.

No Checkerboards in the Pressure Equation

Take the divergence:
\[ \partial_t u + \operatorname{grad} p = f, \quad \operatorname{div} u = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{div}\operatorname{grad} p = \operatorname{div} f \]
Collocated (top): \(p, u\) at the same nodes, \((\operatorname{grad} p)_i = (p_{i+1} - p_{i-1})/2\Delta x\). Then \(\operatorname{div}\operatorname{grad}\) reaches \(p_{i\pm 2}\), and a checkerboard has \(\operatorname{grad} p = 0\).
DEC (bottom): \(p\) on vertices, \(u\) on edges; \(\operatorname{grad} = d_0\), \(\operatorname{div} = -\star_0^{-1} d_0^{\top} \star_1\), so \(\operatorname{div}\operatorname{grad}\) is \((p_{i+1} - 2p_i + p_{i-1})/\Delta x^2\).
With \(p\) on vertices and \(u\) on edges, the pressure equation sees every mode.
[7] F. H. Harlow and J. E. Welch. The Physics of Fluids, 8(12):2182-2189, 1965
17
Where the checkerboard comes from. Momentum sees the pressure only through its gradient, grad p; continuity sees the velocity only through its divergence, div u. Take the divergence of momentum and use div u = 0: the velocity drops out and leaves one equation for the pressure, div grad p = div f, where f is everything else in the momentum equation. A projection method solves exactly this equation every step. So the Laplacian of the pressure equation is div after grad, and its stencil depends on where p and u are stored. Collocated: p and u at the same nodes, grad and div both central differences, (p_{i+1} - p_{i-1}) / 2 dx. Composing them reaches p_{i+2} and p_{i-2} and skips the neighbors, so odd and even nodes never talk. A checkerboard has grad p = 0: it pushes on nothing, continuity cannot see it, and the pressure is undetermined up to it. On a periodic grid that leaves four null modes, top row. DEC: p on vertices, u on edges, grad = d0 and div = minus star0 inverse d0 transpose star1, so div grad is the compact stencil (p_{i+1} - 2 p_i + p_{i-1}) / dx^2. The Hodge stars supply the 1/dx^2: in 1D each star carries one 1/dx; in 2D star1 = 1 and star0 inverse carries both. The same patterns have eigenvalues of minus div grad 0, 4, 4 and 8 in units of 1/dx^2: the checkerboard is now the mode the pressure equation penalizes most. On a grid this is the MAC scheme of Harlow and Welch, and the primal and dual meshes give it on any mesh.

Energy Growth in a Pressure Pulse

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_t u &= -\operatorname{grad} p \\ \partial_t p &= -c^2 \operatorname{div} u \end{aligned} \]
from rest; energy \(\tfrac12\!\int |u|^2 + p^2/c^2\) is conserved
DEC: \(-\operatorname{div} = \operatorname{grad}^*\) and \(d_1 d_0 = 0\). Energy conserved, no vorticity.
Collocated: about 600 growing modes. Energy grows nearly \(10^5\)-fold by \(t = 0.6\).
The collocated energy grows exponentially; the DEC energy is conserved.
18
A flow driven by pressure alone, from rest: the pressure-velocity core of every compressible or low-Mach solver, linear acoustics. The fluid starts at rest with a pressure pulse, and the continuous system conserves the energy one half of u squared plus p squared over c squared, while its velocity stays a gradient, so it never has vorticity. Both runs use the same mesh and the same time integrator, the implicit midpoint rule. DEC: grad = d0 and div = minus star0 inverse d0 transpose star1 satisfy: minus div is the adjoint of grad in the star inner products (diagonal barycentric stars, which are symmetric and positive), so the energy is conserved to six digits and the vorticity stays at 1e-17: an acoustic ring leaves the pulse. Collocated least squares: minus div is not the adjoint of grad, and the curl of the gradient is not zero. The operator has about 600 growing modes, the fastest growing like e^{42 t}; the energy goes from 0.004 to 322 by t = 0.6, nearly a factor of 10^5, and the pressure fills with grid-scale noise. The fastest modes sit at the boundary, where the stencils are one-sided, but holding every boundary node fixed still leaves 518 growing modes. Its pressure Laplacian also has three spurious null modes on this mesh, the unstructured checkerboard.

Cohomology Counts the Holes

closed
\(d_1 u = 0\): no vorticity
exact
\(u = d_0\phi\): a gradient
\[ H^1 = \frac{\ker d_1}{\operatorname{im} d_0}, \qquad \dim H^1 = \#\text{holes} \]
closed flows that are not gradients, counted by integer linear algebra
Its harmonic representative also has \(d_0^{\top}\star_1 h = 0\): it is curl-free and divergence-free, and carries the circulation.
Closed but not exact: the flows that circulate around obstacles.
19
The last identity is about the domain, not the operators. A 1-form u is closed if d1 u = 0: no vorticity in any cell. It is exact if u = d0 phi: a gradient. Every exact form is closed, because d1 d0 = 0. The question is whether every closed form is exact, and the answer depends on the holes. Around a loop that encloses no hole, a closed form has zero circulation by Stokes' theorem; around a hole it can have any circulation, and then it is not a gradient. The quotient, closed forms modulo exact ones, is the first cohomology H1, and its dimension is the number of holes. In DEC this is integer linear algebra on the incidence matrices: dim ker d1 minus rank d0, exactly, on any mesh. The harmonic representative is closed and co-closed: curl-free, divergence-free, and not a gradient. That is the flow that carries circulation, and lift. The plot counts the holes a second way: the Hodge Laplacian on 1-forms has one zero eigenvalue per hole. Square, none; one cylinder, one; two cylinders, two; each at 1e-13 or below against a first nonzero eigenvalue of order one, so the threshold is unambiguous.

The Hodge Decomposition

simply connected\(u = \nabla\phi + \nabla\times\psi\) with holes\(u = \nabla\phi + \nabla\times\psi + h\), with \(\nabla\cdot h = 0\), \(\nabla\times h = 0\), \(h\cdot n = 0\): one \(h\) per hole DEC\(u = d_0\phi + \star_1^{-1} d_1^{\top}\star_2\,\psi + h\), three pieces orthogonal in the \(\star_1\) inner product
With holes, a third piece appears that neither curl nor div can see.
22
The Helmholtz decomposition from vector calculus: on a simply connected domain every velocity field is a gradient plus a curl, u = grad phi + curl psi. The gradient part has no vorticity, the curl part has no divergence, and a projection method for incompressible flow is exactly the step that removes the gradient part. On a domain with holes there is a third piece, the harmonic part h: curl-free, divergence-free, tangent to the boundary, and not a gradient. Its dimension is the number of holes, the cohomology we counted earlier, and on the last slide it was the circulating flows h1 and h2. Because it has neither curl nor divergence, a solver that only controls vorticity and divergence cannot see it; it is fixed by the circulations. In DEC the decomposition is u = d0 phi + star1 inverse d1 transpose star2 psi + h, with psi a 2-form, and the three pieces are orthogonal in the star1 inner product (diagonal barycentric stars, symmetric and positive). Gradient and curl parts are orthogonal because d1 d0 = 0; the gradient part is orthogonal to h because h is co-closed, and the curl part because h is closed. In the continuum the same holds with psi = 0 on the boundary. The figure decomposes a flow on the same two cylinders: a stream, a vortex patch and a vortex around the left cylinder. The pieces are orthogonal to 1e-12, the harmonic piece is curl-free and divergence-free to round-off, the gradient piece has zero circulation around both cylinders, and the harmonic piece carries circulation 5.85 and -1.33 around them.

Mass

Momentum
\(\displaystyle \partial_t U + \textcolor{#E28F41}{\nabla\cdot(u \otimes U)} = -\nabla P(\Theta) + \rho\, g + \mu\, \Delta u\)
Potential temperature
\(\displaystyle \partial_t \Theta + \textcolor{#E28F41}{\nabla\cdot(u\, \Theta)} = \kappa\, \Delta \Theta\)

The hard terms are the advection terms: a divergence of a flux in every equation.
24
The model as it is usually written, in conservative form: mass, momentum and potential temperature. The unknowns are the density rho, the momentum U = rho u and the potential temperature density Theta = rho theta; the pressure comes from Theta through the equation of state. We assume a Newtonian fluid with Stokes' hypothesis, which gives mu times the Laplacian of u plus mu/3 times the gradient of div u, and we drop the second term: the viscous term is mu times the Laplacian of u. Each equation has a divergence of a flux, highlighted, and those advection terms are the hard part numerically. The next slide writes the same equations in exterior calculus.

Compressible Navier–Stokes in Exterior Calculus

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_t \rho + \star d \star U &= 0 \\[6pt] \partial_t U + U \wedge \star d \star u + \mathcal{L}_u U - \tfrac{1}{2}\, \rho\, d \star (u \wedge \star u) &= -dP(\Theta) + \rho\, g + \mu\, \Delta u \\[6pt] \partial_t \Theta + \Theta \wedge \star d \star u + \mathcal{L}_u \Theta &= \kappa\, \Delta \Theta \end{aligned} \]
Compression
\(U \wedge \star d \star u\), the divergence of the flow
Self-advection
\(\mathcal{L}_u U = \iota_u\, dU + d\, \iota_u U\), by Cartan's formula
Energy gradient
\(-\tfrac{1}{2}\, \rho\, d\, |u|^2\), the kinetic energy
Every advection term is a wedge: current work focuses on improving the numerics of wedges.
25
A real model in the words of the dictionary: compressible Navier-Stokes for the density rho, the momentum U = rho u and the potential temperature density Theta, assuming a Newtonian fluid with Stokes' hypothesis and a simplified viscous term. In the momentum and temperature equations the divergence of each flux splits into a compression term, a wedge with star d star u, and a self-advection term, the Lie derivative, which Cartan's formula writes with the interior product and d; the momentum equation keeps a kinetic energy gradient. So every advection term is a wedge, and the wedge is the one operator the numerics have to get right. This is George Rauta's model, and the runs on the next slides solve it.

From Diagram to Simulator

1 · SPECIFY
@decapode
Variables declared as forms; equations in \(d\), \(\star\), \(\partial_t\). The result is a diagram, stored as data.
2 · INFER
infer_types!
Degree and primal or dual flow along the operators. A 1-form added to a 2-form is an error here.
3 · RESOLVE
resolve_overloads!
Generic \(d\) and \(\star\) become \(d_0\), \(\star_1\), dual \(d\); composites fuse into one matrix.
4 · COMPILE
gensim
A Julia function; its matrices are assembled once on a mesh from CombinatorialSpaces.jl.
5 · SOLVE
ODEProblem
Any solver from OrdinaryDiffEq.jl integrates it, on the CPU or the GPU.
The type system is exterior calculus: a mismatched degree is caught before any matrix is built.
[20] L. Morris, A. Baas, J. Arias, M. Gatlin, E. Patterson, and J. Fairbanks. Journal of Computational Science, 81:102345, 2024 · [21]
35
What happens between the macro and the movie. The specification is a diagram, data a program can read. Type inference propagates form degree and primal or dual along the operators, using the rules of exterior calculus: d raises the degree by one, star takes k to n minus k and swaps primal and dual. A model that adds a 1-form to a 2-form is rejected here, before any matrix is built. Overload resolution then picks the concrete operator, star one or dual d one, and fuses composites into a single matrix. Code generation emits a Julia function over a mesh from CombinatorialSpaces, and any ODE solver integrates it, on CPU or GPU.
