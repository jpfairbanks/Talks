Optimal Parts, Suboptimal Whole

A heterogeneous flock is sent to reach a stranded vehicle and observe its surroundings. Before launch it must decide:
Each decision limits the others. Analyzed apart, optimal subsystems compose into a suboptimal whole, and stable ones into an unstable one.
3
This is the motivating scenario from the center's research plan. It couples discrete and continuous optimization, planning and control, and robustness to both uncertain conditions and uncertain communication. For example, dispatching another drone changes the communication delays, which affects stability of the whole flock. Current methods analyze these decisions separately.

One Foundation for Optimization, Dynamics, and Control

The vision: the properties engineers prove about parts survive in the systems they are built into.
Prior work modeled engineering problems in category theory and left the solutions there. We translate solutions back into the engineering formalisms they came from.
4
This is how the center differs from earlier applied category theory work. Step 3 applies to every thrust: results should be usable by engineers who do not know category theory.

Sheaves Are the Common Language

Problems and solutions are both sheaves: continuous ones over a space carry the theory, combinatorial ones over a finite structure carry the computation.
\(\mathbf{Vect}\)
Numerical computing: optimization, dynamics and control as linear algebra
\(\mathbf{Met}\)
Contractive processes: stable dynamics, convergent optimization
\(\mathbf{Sup}\)
Optimal decisions over discrete or nondeterministic search spaces
\(\mathbf{Set}\)
Feasibility: find or enumerate solutions by dynamic programming
Ordered from most structure to least. Most of our current work uses the first row.
[2] J. Hansen and R. Ghrist. Journal of Applied and Computational Topology, 3(4):315–358, 2019
5
Thrust 1 develops this and the other four thrusts use it. Most of our published coordination and optimization work uses vector-space sheaves. Several plan items, including categories of sheaves, open sheaves and quantale-enriched profunctors, extend it to the other rows.

Who Can Track Whom Is a Cohomological Question

Ships Tracking Submarines — Determined
A ship is stuck on the surface, so the target’s three coordinates determine its two. The harmonic extension is unique and the relative cohomology vanishes.
Quadcopters Tracking Ships — Underdetermined
A quadcopter also has an altitude, which nothing it measures about a surface vessel constrains. That spare degree of freedom is the non-vanishing relative cohomology.
Trackability is whether the measurements pin down every degree of freedom the agents have. The cohomology counts the ones they miss.
[5] T. Hanks, C. F. Nino, J. Bou Barcelo, A. Copeland, W. E. Dixon, and J. Fairbanks. European Control Conference, 2026
10
The question the widget on the next slide answers. Ships in the plane tracking submarines in 3D are determined: the targets' coordinates fix the ships'. Quadcopters tracking ships are not: each quadcopter's altitude is left free, and that freedom is the non-vanishing relative cohomology. The widget computes this live for one scenario, and the explorer after it for any sheaf you write down.

Live: Who Can Track Whom

Ships in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), targets in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), linked by the projection that forgets altitude. Pin the ships and four degrees of freedom survive, one altitude per target; pin the targets and none do.
CODAC-Centers/SheafApps · also at codac-centers.github.io/software/multi-domain-tracking · [5]
11
Click into the widget to use it: drag an agent, toggle pins, scrub diffusion time. Click outside the frame to get the arrow keys back for the slides. The degrees-of-freedom readout is the dimension of H⁰ for the pinned set, which is the cohomological answer to who can track whom from the ECC paper. Runs offline; nothing is fetched.

The Cellular Sheaf Explorer

Six worked examples, a source editor for writing your own sheaf, and the spectrum and energy readouts. In the browser, with nothing to install.
12
The picker in the top right has six sheaves, simplest first: braced square, bare square, degenerate triangle, offset formation, infeasible cycle, multi-domain tracking. Bare square is the quickest to show: deleting the brace adds one degree of freedom, and sweeping it shears the square into a rhombus. This is the tool for engineers who have never seen a sheaf.
