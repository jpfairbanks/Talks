Two Design Problems Share a Variable

Designing a drone: the wing team picks wing size \(x_1\) and battery \(x_2\); the motor team picks battery \(x_2\) and motor \(x_3\).
\[ \min_{x_1, x_2, x_3}\; f(x_1, x_2) + g(x_2, x_3) \qquad\text{optimal when}\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} + \frac{\partial g}{\partial x_2} = 0 \]
The shared battery is the wire. Each team computes its own derivative; only the battery terms have to be exchanged.
Each team keeps its own cost. The drawing says which variables they must agree on.
6
A drone design split between two teams. The wing team trades wing size against battery weight; the motor team trades battery weight against motor size. Each team has its own cost. The battery is the wire between them: the combined cost is the sum of the two, and each team's part is unchanged. At the best design the two teams' derivatives in the battery cancel, so each team can work on its own cost as long as they keep exchanging that one number. That exchange is what lets a big problem be solved in pieces.

Rates Add Up, and So Do Models

Two heaters in one room: model \(v\) changes heater A \(x_1\) and the room \(x_2\); model \(u\) changes the room \(x_2\) and heater B \(x_3\).
\[ \dot x_1 = v(x_1, x_2)_1, \qquad \dot x_2 = v(x_1, x_2)_2 + u(x_2, x_3)_1, \qquad \dot x_3 = u(x_2, x_3)_2 \]
Each box is a vector field, a small model of how things change. Where two boxes share a variable, their rates add.
The drawing is the same as on the last slide. There it added costs; here it adds rates.
7
The same drawing, read a second way. Two heaters in one room: heater A's model says how its temperature and the room's change, heater B's model does the same, and the room's temperature changes at the sum of the two rates. Each box is a small model of how things change, written as a vector field; the shared variable is the room, and the combined model adds the two rates there. The drawing tells the computer to do this for any number of pieces.

Physics Laws Are Building Blocks

For a concentration \(C\) carried by a flow \(V\):
Diffusion
\(\phi_1 = -k\,\nabla C\) down the slope
Advection
\(\phi_2 = C\,V\) carried along
Superposition
\(\phi = \phi_1 + \phi_2\)
Conservation
\(\partial_t C = -\nabla\cdot\phi\)
Wired together:
\[ \partial_t C = k\,\Delta C - \nabla\cdot(C\,V) \]
\(\Delta = \nabla\cdot\nabla\) is the Laplacian. It compares each point with the average of the points around it.
The laws, one diagram each
The diagrams wired together
9
Four laws from heat and mass transfer, for a concentration C: dye in water, smoke in air. Temperature obeys the same equations, with Fourier's law in place of Fick's. Each law is drawn as a small diagram: the circles are quantities, the arrows are operations like "take the gradient". The diagrams leave out the two minus signs, which cancel. Wiring the four together gives the equation at the bottom without anyone deriving it by hand. Point at the Laplacian: it is diffusion, and it comes back in the robot part.

Three Classic Fluid Experiments

Wind over water: layers curl into waves
Heavy fluid falls through light
A warm bubble rises
All three solve the Navier–Stokes equations, \(\partial_t u + (u\cdot\nabla)u = -\nabla p + \nu\,\Delta u\), with buoyancy added for the last two. The \(\nu\,\Delta u\) term is the Laplacian again: viscosity is diffusion of momentum.
11
Three classic fluid experiments, each simulated from a model built the way the last slide showed. Left: two layers sliding past each other curl into waves, the same instability that makes waves on a windy lake. Middle: heavy fluid resting on light fluid falls through it in fingers. Right: a warm bubble rises and rolls up. All three are the Navier-Stokes equations, the momentum balance for a fluid, with buoyancy added for the last two; nu times the Laplacian of u is viscosity, the same diffusion term as before, now spreading momentum instead of heat.

Agreeing with Your Neighbors

Each robot \(i\) talks only to its neighbors \(N_i\), and follows one rule:
Move toward your neighbors. Repeat.
\[ \dot x_i = \sum_{j \in N_i} (x_j - x_i) \]
Stack all sixteen robots into one vector \(x\):
\[ \dot x = -L\,x, \qquad L = D - A \]
\(L\) is the graph Laplacian: degrees \(D\) minus links \(A\). It is the heat equation, run on the communication graph.
The robots agree for the same reason temperature evens out.
14
Sixteen robots in a ring; each one can only talk to the two beside it. Every robot runs the same rule: move toward your neighbors. Stack the sixteen equations and you get one matrix equation with the graph Laplacian, which is the heat equation from the last part with the mesh replaced by the communication graph. The robots' values even out the way temperature does, and that is why they end up agreeing with nobody in charge.

Different Robots, Different Views

  • ●A drone sees from above, a boat sees the surface, a submarine measures its depth
  • ●Their states live in different spaces, so \(x_j - x_i\) means nothing
  • ●On each link \(e = ij\), translate both into a shared space first:
\[ \mathcal{F}_{i \unlhd e}\, x_i \;-\; \mathcal{F}_{j \unlhd e}\, x_j \]
The translations \(\mathcal{F}\) form a sheaf. Using its Laplacian in the same rule,
\[ \dot x = -L_{\mathcal{F}}\, x, \]
the team converges to a state that every link agrees on.
15
The averaging rule assumes every robot measures the same kind of thing, so that x_j minus x_i makes sense. A real team is mixed: a drone sees from above, a boat sees the surface, a submarine measures its depth. Before two of them can compare notes, each translates what it knows into the terms of the link between them. The collection of those translations, one pair for every link, is a sheaf, and swapping the graph Laplacian for the sheaf Laplacian gives the same kind of rule. The team settles on a state that every link agrees on.
